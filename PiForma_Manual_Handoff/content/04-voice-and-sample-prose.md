# Style sheet: owner's voice in a vintage manual

## Desired register

Approachable, modestly professional, gently cordial, a bit self-aware, occasionally dry or teasing. The owner speaks conversationally and wants his perspective to show, but not rambling transcription. The guide should be informative first. Think 'actual useful Macintosh manual by a hobbyist who has spent too long making this thing work,' not 'marketing brochure' or overproduced nostalgia parody.

- Direct sentences; explain what the reader will see, hear and touch.
- Early chapters teach; later chapters satisfy curiosity and provide detail.
- A little humor belongs in introductions, cautions and support note, not every paragraph.
- Keep observations honest: 'sometimes it needs to be plugged in briefly' is better than a speculative electrical explanation.
- Some period flavor is welcome ('Before you begin', 'Taking a closer look', 'Important') but do not lift extended Apple prose.
- Do not assert that this is an Apple-manufactured Macintosh, a modern Mac, safety-certified equipment or a supported commercial product.
- Never write an entire chapter only to fill a page. Do not force equal word counts with Apple's originals.
- Avoid modern software-marketing terms such as 'seamless', 'powerful experience', 'revolutionary', and filler like 'journey'.
- Address the reader as 'you', but let the owner's humor come through in brief parenthetical comments.

## Sample opening: Welcome

Welcome to the Macintosh PiForma.

The PiForma looks a little like something that might have sat on a desk in the mid-1980s, but the insides are rather different. It began with an old Apple monitor, a Raspberry Pi, and an unreasonable willingness to make things more complicated than they strictly needed to be. The result is a portable little computer for games, familiar old software, and a few experiments that seemed like good ideas at the time.

This guide explains how to unpack it, start it, use the controls, and put everything away without having to remember which piece of foam goes where. Later chapters explain how it works and what was used to build it. You don't need to know much about computers to begin, although some of the technical sections may reward a little curiosity.

## Sample introduction: About this manual

Chapter 1, 'Getting Started,' shows you how to remove the PiForma and its accessories from the carrying bag, connect the things that need connecting, and start the computer. It also explains how to get everything back into the bag, which is a bit more particular than taking it out.

Chapter 2, 'Inside the Macintosh PiForma,' introduces the monitor, Raspberry Pi, battery, keyboard, mouse, speakers, controls and connectors.

Chapter 3, 'How the Macintosh PiForma Works,' explains the desktop, applications, games and the controls used to switch between them.

Chapter 4, 'Caring for Your Macintosh PiForma,' offers a few suggestions for keeping the computer and its traveling companions in one piece.

Appendix A supplies the technical details for anyone interested in the wiring, software and other parts of the build.

## Sample: unpacking

Open the carrying bag with the bag standing upright. The first thing you should see is a small brown accessory pouch. Remove it and set it aside. Inside are the two Nintendo 64 controllers, the USB hub, and the power cable.

Below the pouch are the mouse and the folding stand. Take those out next, followed by this guide, the mouse pad, and the velvet-covered foam board. The PiForma rests underneath with its screen facing up. The keyboard is tucked alongside it, so take care not to catch the keys as you remove the computer.

The PiForma's carrying handle may be hidden against the inside front wall of the bag. Slip a hand between that wall and the top of the computer until you can pull the handle free. If the computer doesn't come out easily, lay the bag on its back first. There's no prize for wrestling it out upright.

## Sample: battery oddity

**Important**  If nothing happens when you press the power button, check the battery before assuming something has broken. The button inside the top opening can be pressed using the outside plunger. This displays the battery's charge level and may also wake it up. Occasionally the battery needs a brief connection to external power even though it appears to be charged. Try that before taking anything apart.

## Sample: the support department

### Service and support

There isn't any. Sorry.

The Macintosh PiForma is a one-off experimental project, assembled for enjoyment rather than manufacture or sale. There is no service department, help desk, or helpful person in a tie waiting by the phone. If something needs repair, it'll probably come back to the person who built it, assuming he's in the mood. Treat it kindly and perhaps neither of us will have to find out.
