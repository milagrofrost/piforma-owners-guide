# Welcome to the Macintosh PiForma

<!-- evidence: owner-current H04; repo-verified D-README D-HARDWARE -->
Welcome to the Macintosh PiForma.

This little computer began in the basement of Apple headquarters, which is remarkable, because Apple headquarters did not have a basement. A rogue engineer came across an Apple IIc Monitor down there, emptied it, and Frankensteined a computer into the case from whatever parts were lying around, several of which had not, strictly speaking, been invented yet.

<!-- evidence: owner-current OWNER-TALK-2026-10-19 -->
The work went on under the nose of the vice president of the day, a man with firm opinions about customers opening their computers. In short, they shouldn’t. The engineer was in no hurry to show him a Macintosh with easy access to its insides, running open-source software and other people’s good ideas.

Then, in 1985, the vice president was let go, and the mood changed. The CEO turned out to be rather more open-minded about unconventional ideas than anyone had expected, and saw in the PiForma an experiment in something nobody had a name for yet: a Macintosh clone, built from someone else’s parts. Clones would not officially arrive for another ten years; this one simply got there first. The project came up out of the basement, and the PiForma was given the green light.

Only one PiForma was ever made, and this is it. Its story is almost entirely unknown; you may be the first person ever to read it.

Inside, the old monitor’s case holds a modern display and a battery-powered computer, with a familiar-looking desktop, games, and a few diversions that have earned their place mostly by being amusing. Its name is a small joke; its carrying bag is a surprisingly serious undertaking. You can use it without knowing how the wiring works, although you will want to know which way the keyboard goes back into the bag.

This guide explains what the PiForma does, what is inside it and how it works, laid out the way Apple laid out the manuals that came with its own computers. It starts with the things you need to touch, connect, and switch on. Later chapters explain the parts and programs behind the appearance. There is no required course in Raspberry Pi administration before your first game.

# About this manual

<!-- evidence: editorial-advice OUTLINE -->
**Chapter 1, “Getting Started,”** explains how to unpack the computer, arrange the equipment, start up, and stop, and what to do if it won’t start. It also puts everything back into the bag in the right order. Taking things out is easier than remembering where they belonged.

**Chapter 2, “Inside the Macintosh PiForma,”** introduces the monitor shell, Raspberry Pi, display, battery, controls, keyboard, mouse, speakers, and bag. It explains what the parts do before getting into the finer details.

**Chapter 3, “How the Macintosh PiForma Works,”** introduces the desktop, launcher, menu bar, games, and other programs. It distinguishes the computer’s physical controls from the menus on its screen.

**Chapter 4, “Caring for Your Macintosh PiForma,”** covers handling, cleaning, charging, storage, and transport. It includes the brief but comprehensive description of the service department.

**Appendix A** collects technical information and points to the project documentation. **Appendix B** helps you find a useful check when something goes wrong. The **glossary** explains unfamiliar terms used in the guide.

Start with Chapter 1 for your first session. Return to Chapter 3 when you want to work with programs and files, or to Appendix B when the computer has other plans.
